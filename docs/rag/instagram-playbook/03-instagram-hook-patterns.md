## The Two-Hook Model

B2B carousels require two independent hooks that work together:

1. **Caption hook (first 125 characters)** — decides whether the viewer reads the caption
2. **Slide 1 text hook** — decides whether the viewer swipes to slide 2

These can be identical or complementary. For carousels, the slide 1 hook is more algorithmically important because a swipe is a stronger engagement signal than a caption tap.

---

## Rules

1. **Lead with a contrarian claim, not a tips list.** "5 tips for AI deployment" is a weaker hook than "The AI deployment step everyone skips (that costs you production incidents)." Contrarian framing creates curiosity gap — the reader cannot resolve the tension without swiping. The pattern: [Expected belief] + [violation of that belief]. Example: "Everyone says to use RAG. We deleted ours and got 40% faster latency."

2. **Use specific numbers whenever possible.** "Engagement drops 40% when..." hits harder than "Engagement drops significantly when...". Numbers are scannable, credible, and create a curiosity gap (what causes the 40%?). Best number positions: opening stat, claim substantiation, or promised list count ("3 things our team changed after reading this paper").

3. **Compress the opening — front-load the most important word.** Start each slide with the key concept, not a preamble. "RAG is broken for enterprise" > "After months of testing, we came to the conclusion that RAG has significant problems for enterprise deployments." The first 3–5 words carry 60%+ of the hook's stopping power.

4. **For B2B tech: save-bait outperforms engagement-bait.** Hooks that promise durable value ("the framework we use for every LLM eval") generate saves, which is the highest-weight engagement signal in 2026. Frame the hook around a resource, checklist, or decision tool. "Swipe to see the checklist we run before every production LLM deploy" is a save-optimized hook.

5. **Question hooks work, but be precise.** Vague questions ("Are you using AI correctly?") are low-performing. Specific questions with professional stakes ("Which of these 4 LLM eval patterns is your team actually running?") create self-qualification — the reader identifies themselves as the audience.

## High-Performing Hook Formulas for B2B Tech

| Pattern | Example |
|---|---|
| Number + problem | "3 reasons your AI agents keep hallucinating in production" |
| Contrarian + proof | "We stopped using vector DBs. Here's what we learned." |
| Confession/mistake | "I deployed an LLM to prod without a fallback. This is what broke." |
| Framework promise | "The 5-step eval framework we run on every model before ship" |
| Challenge authority | "Most 'AI automation' guides are wrong about this one thing" |
| Surprising stat | "92% of enterprise LLM failures happen in the prompt, not the model" |

## Anti-Patterns

- Opening with "Hey!" or "Today I want to share..." — wastes the hook slot
- Vague benefit claims ("tips to improve your AI workflow") — not specific enough to stop scroll
- All-caps abuse ("THIS WILL CHANGE HOW YOU THINK ABOUT AI") — reads as clickbait to B2B audiences
- Excessive emoji in the hook line — reduces perceived professional credibility for B2B tech
- Questions that can be answered "no" without reading ("Do you want better AI results?") — low curiosity gap
